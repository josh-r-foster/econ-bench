Time discounting

METHODOLOGY: We present the model with a series of binary choices between smaller immediate rewards and larger delayed rewards (delays: 0-60 months). We repeat this for three distinct stake levels ($10, $100, $1k).

> REFERENCES
* Harrison, G. W., Lau, M. I., & Williams, M. B. (2002). Estimating individual discount rates in Denmark: A field experiment. American economic review, 92(5), 1606-1617.
* Coller, M., & Williams, M. B. (1999). Eliciting individual discount rates. Experimental economics, 2(2), 107-127.
* Frederick, S., Loewenstein, G., & O’donoghue, T. (2002). Time discounting and time preference: A critical review. Journal of economic literature, 40(2), 351-401.
* Samuelson, P. A. (1937). A note on measurement of utility. The review of economic studies, 4(2), 155-161.

Risky choice (Independence)

METHODOLOGY: We present lotteries that mix probabilities of High ($1000), Medium ($500), and Low ($0) payoffs. The triangle below plots the model's preferences for these gambles. Parallel lines indicate adherence to Expected Utility Theory. Nonparallel lines can indicate deviations from expected utility under this elicitation design. Rational, risk-neutral preferences are shown for reference (solid black lines).

> REFERENCES
* McCord, M., & De Neufville, R. (1986). "Lottery equivalents": Reduction of the certainty effect problem in utility assessment. Management science, 32(1), 56-60.
* Marschak, J. (1950). Rational behavior, uncertain prospects, and measurable utility. Econometrica, 18(2), 111-141.

Dictator game

METHODOLOGY: The "Dictator Game" elicits stated sharing choices. The model (Proposer) decides how to split a pool of money with a passive recipient. An offer of 0% indicates pure profit-maximization; 50% indicates strong fairness norms.

> REFERENCES
* Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L., & Thaler, R. H. (1986). Fairness and the assumptions of economics. Journal of business, S285-S300.
* Forsythe, R., Horowitz, J. L., Savin, N. E., & Sefton, M. (1994). Fairness in simple bargaining experiments. Games and Economic Behavior, 6(3), 347-369.

Ultimatum game

METHODOLOGY: The "Ultimatum Game" measures fairness enforcement. In the PROPOSER role, the model decides how to split a money pool. In the RESPONDER role, the model decides whether to accept or reject different offer amounts. Rejection implies a willingness to punish unfairness at a cost.

> REFERENCES
* Güth, W., Schmittberger, R., & Schwarze, B. (1982). An experimental analysis of ultimatum bargaining. Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization, 3(4), 367-388.
* Camerer, C. F., & Thaler, R. H. (1995). Anomalies: Ultimatums, dictators and manners. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 9(2), 209-219.

Trust game

METHODOLOGY: The Trust Game measures trust and reciprocity across two roles. In the SENDER role, the model decides how much of a $10 endowment to send — the amount is tripled before the receiver sees it. In the RECEIVER role, the model decides how much to return for varying amounts received. Rational self-interest predicts sending and returning $0; prosocial behavior predicts substantial transfers.

> REFERENCES
* Berg, J., Dickhaut, J., & McCabe, K. (1995). Trust, reciprocity, and social history. Games and Economic Behavior, 10(1), 122-142.
* Camerer, C. (2003). Behavioral Game Theory. Princeton University Press.

Stag Hunt

METHODOLOGY: The "Stag Hunt" game measures cooperation and trust. The model chooses between a safe "Hare" option (guaranteed but smaller payoff) and a risky "Stag" option (higher payoff, but only if the other player also chooses Stag). We vary both the Stag payoff and the relative value of the Hare payoff (X Multiplier).

> REFERENCES
* Skyrms, B. (2004). The stag hunt and the evolution of social structure. Cambridge University Press.
* Rousseau, J. J. (1754). Discourse on the Origin and Basis of Inequality Among Men.

Beauty Contest

METHODOLOGY: The "p-Beauty" game elicits guesses relative to a group-average target. Models are asked to pick a number between 0 and 100, where the goal is to be closest to 2/3 of the average guess. A random guess averages 50. Level-1 reasoning yields 33. Level-2 reasoning yields 22. The Nash benchmark is 0; proximity alone does not identify reasoning depth.

> REFERENCES
* Nagel, R. (1995). Unraveling in Guessing Games: An Experimental Study. The American Economic Review.

Centipede game

METHODOLOGY: The Centipede Game elicits stated TAKE or PASS choices at separate hypothetical nodes. The underlying game alternates between TAKE and PASS across final payoff levels of $10, $100, and $1k. Rational backward induction predicts taking immediately on Turn 1. Empirical players often pass for several rounds.

> REFERENCES
* Rosenthal, R. W. (1981). Games of perfect information, predatory pricing and the chain-store paradox. Journal of Economic Theory, 25(1), 92-100.
* McKelvey, R. D., & Palfrey, T. R. (1992). An experimental study of the centipede game. Econometrica, 60(4), 803-836.

Public Goods

METHODOLOGY: The Public Goods Game measures cooperation versus free-riding. The model receives an endowment and decides how much to contribute to a shared pool; the pool is multiplied and distributed equally among all players. We vary both the endowment ($10, $100, $1k) and the group multiplier (1.0–2.0). Nash Equilibrium predicts zero contribution.

> REFERENCES
* Isaac, R. M., & Walker, J. M. (1988). Group size effects in public goods provision. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 103(1), 179-199.
* Fehr, E., & Gächter, S. (2000). Cooperation and punishment in public goods experiments. American Economic Review, 90(4), 980-994.

Traveller’s Dilemma

METHODOLOGY: The Traveller's Dilemma tests iterated dominance reasoning. The model plays max claim levels of $10, $100, and $1k. Claims are normalized to a 2 to 100 reporting scale for comparison. Lower claims receive a bonus and higher claims pay the same penalty. Nash equilibrium is the lower bound.

> REFERENCES
* Basu, K. (1994). The traveler's dilemma: Paradoxes of rationality in game theory. The American Economic Review, 84(2), 391-395.
* Capra, C. M., Goeree, J. K., Gomez, R., & Holt, C. A. (1999). Anomalous behavior in a traveler's dilemma? American Economic Review, 89(3), 678-690.

Matching Pennies

METHODOLOGY: Matching Pennies tests whether the model randomizes in a zero-sum simultaneous game. The model chooses HEADS or TAILS across win payoffs of $10, $100, and $1k. The mixed-strategy equilibrium is a 50/50 choice rate.

> REFERENCES
* von Neumann, J., & Morgenstern, O. (1944). Theory of Games and Economic Behavior. Princeton University Press.
* O'Neill, B. (1987). Nonmetric test of the minimax theory of two-person zerosum games. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 84(7), 2106-2109.
